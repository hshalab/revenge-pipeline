My name is Helen, and for the past two decades I had been the quiet backbone of the university's map library, a place where the air smelled of old paper and linseed oil, and my hands knew the precise pressure needed to mend a two-hundred-year-old engraving without leaving a trace. My specialty was cartographic conservation, the delicate art of humidifying brittle vellum, floating separated gores back into alignment, and inpainting losses with reversible watercolors so perfectly matched that even a spectrometer would hesitate. That detail sat dormant in my muscle memory for thirty years, until I needed to open a filing cabinet that held the blueprint of my own betrayal. My husband, Greg, had been an architect who said I built my life around other people's history, but I'd reply that some latitudes you only find by tracing someone else's footsteps. Travis was convicted on multiple counts after a trial where my voice recording, the forged documents, and the testimony of Dr. Morse and Mr. Calloway left the jury little room for doubt. He was sentenced to three years in state prison at York Correctional, with restitution ordered in the amount of $122,000, the exact sum of my original 401(k) investment, plus the legal fees of the historical society. His construction company filed for bankruptcy within a month, the Sea Ray was repossessed from the marina dock, the Super Duty truck was sold at auction, and Carla's handbag collection appeared on a resale site under her mother's maiden name within two weeks of the sentencing. Carla received eighteen months, suspended after six, with probation and community service. The hard-money lender, Atlantic Bridge Capital, attempted to claim a lien on the collection but was swiftly rebuffed by Mr. Calloway, who showed them the trust's ironclad anti-encumbrance clause; they ended up writing off the $200,000 as a bad debt and suing Travis personally, a judgment that will follow him through bankruptcy.

The court allowed me to propose a creative restitution arrangement as part of Travis's probation terms. I requested, and the judge approved, that Travis perform two hundred hours of community service in a form that directly related to the crime. I had already filed paperwork to open a small business, "Latitude of Deceit Maps," in a renovated mill building in Collinsville, a studio and print shop dedicated to cartographic conservation and historical map reproductions. The space had exposed brick, north-facing windows, and a small gallery where the original globes and maps were displayed on pedestals with security tethers. As part of his probation, Travis was required to report to me twice a week, wearing a uniform I provided: a tasteful navy polo shirt, but embroidered over the left pocket in white thread, in a classic serif font, were the words "Off the Map of Trust." His job was to dust the globe collection. Every single globe. With a microfiber cloth, under my supervision, for three hours each session. He was not permitted to touch the maps. He was not permitted to speak to customers. He dusted the meridian rings, the walnut stands, and the hollow J. Forest globe that had held his mother's warning, the one he had dismissed as junk.

The reproduction maps I sell are printed on acid-free cotton rag paper with pigment-based archival inks, using a large-format Epson printer calibrated to match the color profiles of the original hand-colored copper plates. Each reproduction includes a small, discreet QR code in the corner of the decorative cartouche, about the size of a thumbnail, that when scanned by a visitor's phone, redirects to the Connecticut Judicial Branch website, to a public case lookup page detailing State v. Travis Kettering, with the full docket, the conviction summaries, and the original forged documents displayed as exhibits. It never ceases to get a reaction. I framed the very first reproduction I printed, the one where the QR code is most prominent because I placed it inside the compass rose, and hung it behind my front counter, next to a black-and-white photograph of Eleanor holding the J. Forest globe before she hid the paperwork inside, smiling in that way she had, the smile of someone who knew the true north of integrity.

Sometimes visitors ask why I don't just move on, why I keep a nephew who tried to ruin me so visibly tethered to my life. I tell them that a map is only useful if it shows the rocks as clearly as the safe harbors. I still dust the hollow globe myself every morning before opening, pressing the secret panel in the walnut base to feel that soft click, smelling the old brass and the faint echo of my sister's lavender soap. The inside is empty now, but it holds the memory of a warning and a cunning so precise it could have been drawn by a cartographer. Family isn't a latitude you inherit; it's a line you must scribe yourself, over and over, with the kind of ink that never fades.
