My name is Lydia Harrow. I'm fifty-three years old, and I've been a widow for almost nine years. My husband Mark left me a pension and a small house with a kitchen that faced the sunrise, and after he was gone I filled every quiet morning measuring sodium hydroxide into distilled water and watching the heat bloom through the glass. Cold-process soapmaking is more chemistry than craft, and I loved it for exactly that reason: you weigh palm kernel oil and unrefined shea butter on a jeweler's scale, you bring them to exactly one hundred ten degrees Fahrenheit, and when the lye solution meets the fats the whole mixture turns opaque and leaves a ribbon on the surface, a trace that holds for three full seconds before sinking. That ribbon meant the molecules were rearranging themselves into something that could clean, and after Mark died, watching that transformation was the only thing that rearranged me.

My son Daniel married a woman named Lila Brentwood three years after Mark's funeral. I paid for the wedding because her own parents refused, a small ceremony at a restored gristmill with two hundred white tea roses and a string quartet that Daniel hummed along to while Lila posed for photographs she later airbrushed into porcelain smoothness. Daniel was a forensic accountant, the kind who spent eleven months a year untangling fraud on the road, and because he understood the architecture of deception better than anyone I've ever known, he insisted we notarize our partnership at the county clerk's office. Lila and I signed a fifty-fifty operating agreement for an artisanal cold-process bath and body brand we called Lavender Haven. I liquidated every last cent of Mark's pension, one hundred twenty-three thousand dollars. I paid for the stainless steel triple boiler, the commercial curing racks, the essential oil barrels, and the first run of twelve hundred custom boxes printed with a sprig of lavender that looked like it had been drawn by a hand that actually cared. Because Daniel kept seventeen hotel keycards in his glove compartment at any given time, I wrote an extra provision into the contract: Lila and I each received a lifetime supply of the lavender-chamomile bars made from the very first batch, poured in a six-cavity silicone mold with a honeycomb texture that left a hexagonal imprint on every bar. I remember scraping the trace into each cavity with a rubber spatula, the scent of Roman chamomile and Bulgarian lavender so thick it clung to my hair for two days. That detail will matter later.

Daniel died on a wet Tuesday evening in October, hydroplaning on Interstate 74 into a concrete divider that had claimed eleven other drivers in the past decade. The state trooper who came to my door was younger than Daniel, and he handed me a sealed plastic bag with a wallet, a phone that would never ring again, and a hotel receipt from Ohio that still smelled faintly of the coffee he'd spilled on it that morning. Before the funeral flowers had even begun to wilt, Lila arrived at my house with a man I'd never met, a financial planner named Troy Mercer whose teeth were so bleached they looked like they'd been laminated under a hot press. Lila was weeping, but it was a clean, television kind of weeping, all symmetrical tears and no swollen eyelids. She hugged me with one arm and used the other to gesture at my living room, saying, "We need to get you organized, Mama Lydia," a name she had never once used while my son was alive.

Within thirty hours, they had filled four cardboard cartons with Daniel's possessions. His childhood report cards, the brass desk clock I'd given him for his twenty-first birthday, his collection of forensic accounting journals, a shoebox of loose change from a dozen countries, and a wooden soap mold he had crafted in high school woodshop. The mold was walnut, about twelve inches by four inches, with a fitted lid and dovetail joints so fine you could not slide a piece of paper between them. I'd forgotten he had even kept it. Lila picked it up, turned it over in her hands, and said to Troy, "This thing smells like rancid tallow. It's trash." I padded around during the early morning hours, usually between four-thirty and six, when Troy and Lila were still in their upstairs bedroom and the house was silent except for the hum of the refrigerator compressor. I needed proof, something tangible that could not be dismissed as a grieving mother's confusion. I needed the documents in that steel filing cabinet. It was a Hon four-drawer vertical filing cabinet in the corner of the home office, battleship gray, with a Medeco pin-tumbler lock on the top drawer that controlled all four. Medeco locks use rotating pins and sidebar grooves, far more complex than a standard Kwikset or Schlage. I hadn't touched a tension wrench in thirty years, but my father was a locksmith who ran a small shop called Harrow Keys with a neon sign shaped like a skeleton key that blinked red in the window. Every summer from age thirteen until I left for college, I sat on a stool beside him, rekeying pin tumblers and learning the feel of a binding pin, that tiny increase in resistance that tells you the driver pin is crossing the shear line. I knew that a Medeco lock would not yield to a simple rake; I needed a half-diamond pick that I could use to lift each pin individually, and I needed to feel the rotational resistance of the sidebar.

I did not have my father's old kit, so I made my own inside the cottage. I retrieved a thin stainless steel windshield wiper insert from a discarded wiper blade I found in their garage, a strip of spring steel about a quarter-inch wide and twelve inches long. For a tension wrench, I used the flat spring steel spine from a manila folder clip, bending the last eighth of an inch into a right-angle foot. It took me two evenings, working by the light of the cottage bathroom vanity, testing the feel of the pick against the underside of a bathroom drawer lock I had brought from the cottage door.

On a Thursday night when Lila and Troy were at some networking dinner, I walked into the home office wearing cargo pants with deep pockets, my father's old muscle memory returning like a song half-remembered. I inserted the tension wrench into the bottom of the keyway and applied gentle clockwise torque, then slid the half-diamond pick to the back of the lock and began lifting each pin, feeling for the tiny click that meant the driver pin had set above the shear line. A Medeco sidebar lock requires you to set the pins and then rotate them individually to align the sidebar grooves, but I had spent a summer practicing on a Medeco deadbolt my father kept for training, and I knew the trick: after setting the pins, you release tension very slightly and feel for the sidebar to drop. On the fourth pin, I felt the telltale shift, the core rotated a single degree, and the plug turned with a satisfying clunk. I had the drawer open in a little under twelve minutes.
