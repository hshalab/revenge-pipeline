The back reads "Rooted in Fraud" in reflective silver letters, and the front pocket is embroidered with the probate case number, 24CV007891, in white thread. He repots seedlings into fresh sphagnum moss, scrubs algae from the misting nozzles with an old toothbrush, and sorts fir bark growing medium into bins by grade size. He doesn't speak to me unless I ask a direct question, and I rarely do. I watch him sometimes from the doorway, and I feel nothing but a clean, cool indifference, like the temperature inside the fogging room. Passersby at the Sunday farmers market, where we sell small orchid starts in clay pots, sometimes snap photos of him in that vest. I don't stop them. Every orchid we sell now carries a small laser printed plant tag that lists the hybrid name, its care instructions, and at the bottom in a six point font, the line: "Probate Case No. 24CV007891." Customers sometimes ask what it means. I tell them it's a reminder that some flowers bloom best after the rot is cut away, and that a well written contract is a more powerful fertilizer than any smile.

Dad's little glass cloche now sits on a lighted shelf in the visitor center, restored and polished, with a brass plaque that explains how one man's foresight saved a lifetime of careful work from vultures. I still deliver fresh orchid arrangements to the altar at St. Mark's every Sunday, just like the agreement demanded. Father Michael says the altar hasn't smelled so sweet in decades, and the old ladies in the front pew have started a rumor that the orchids actually last longer than any cut flowers they've ever seen. I think Dad would be proud, and on quiet afternoons when the misters kick on and the glass panels throw rainbows across the stone floor, I can almost hear him humming among the cordyline roots.
