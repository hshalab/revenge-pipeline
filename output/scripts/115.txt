My name is Evelyn Dorsey. I’m fifty-four years old, a retired medical records supervisor from Calloway Regional Hospital, and for twenty-eight years I verified signatures on surgical consent forms, insurance claims, and power-of-attorney documents. I managed the hospital’s transition from paper to digital records, training nurses on electronic signature pads while also teaching them that a stylus stroke is still a signature, and a crime if forged. I knew that a signature is a fingerprint of the soul, the pressure of a pen, the angle of a loop, the tiny tremble in a terminal stroke, and I learned to spot a forgery the way a bloodhound catches scent. My late husband, Leonard Coleman, was the quiet architect of a historic Victorian funeral home that had served our town since 1904. The building had a turret, a wraparound porch, and a carriage house we converted into the embalming room. Leonard was the kind of undertaker who kept his books in meticulous leather ledgers, who still used fountain pen ink for death certificates, and who believed that dignity in death was the last honest currency we had. He had one son from his first marriage, Mitchell, and I had raised the boy since he was fifteen, paid for his mortuary science degree with my savings, and stood beside him when Leonard died of a sudden stroke at sixty-one. Before the dirt had even settled on Leonard’s grave, I used his ninety-two thousand dollar life insurance payout to buy a fifty percent stake in Coleman and Son Funeral Home, because Mitchell said he needed capital to modernize the embalming room, replace the old porcelain embalming table with stainless steel, and I thought family meant forever. I kept that article framed in the parlor.

Then, the community service began. Mitchell reported to the cemetery every Saturday at dawn, wearing a high-visibility orange shirt that I had a local print shop produce in bulk. I designed it myself: the front bore the Eulogy of Lies Memorials logo, and the back read “6 Feet Under Fraud” in bold lettering with a small shovel icon and the court case number. I ensured the orange fabric met ANSI high-visibility standards so there could be no safety objection. The cemetery supervisor, a man named Gus, told me Mitchell protested, but Gus said, “You wear what I tell you or you go back to the judge.” So Mitchell digs graves by hand. No backhoe. Just a long-handled spade, a pickaxe for the rocky soil, and a plot number. He has to measure the dimensions with a collapsible ruler, square the walls with a string line and a level, and sometimes, when the ground is frozen in January, he pounds through frost with a breaker bar while mourners watch from the chapel windows across the lawn. He digs to a depth of four and a half feet for a standard liner, shovelful by shovelful, and the dirt piles beside each new grave like a slow tally of his penance. I do not stand over him gloating; I do not need to. The work speaks its own sermon.

At Eulogy of Lies Memorials, we handle every service with a transparency that has become our hallmark. Our general price list is printed on the back of every program, itemized down to the cost of the embalming fluid ounce and the prayer card stock. We include a section called “The Dignity Assurance,” outlining exactly how pre-need funds are held in trust. But the most requested feature, and the one that gives me quiet satisfaction, is the memorial program template I designed. Under the closing hymn title, right after the committal prayer, there is a small line in ten-point serif type that reads: “This funeral home is owned and operated by Evelyn Dorsey, sole trustee. Mitchell Coleman, former partner, is a convicted perjurer and forger, Case No. CR-2024-03871, Bancroft County Superior Court.” Every family is asked if they wish to include the notice. Most do, some with a knowing nod, some with a request to enlarge the font for the back page. It has become a small piece of local lore, a testimony that even in the funeral business, accountability buries shame.

I still visit Leonard’s grave every Sunday. I bring fresh white roses because they were his favorite, and I sit on the marble bench we installed nearby. I tell him about the new cooling slab that uses an eco-friendly refrigerant, the grief counseling room we opened in the old carriage house with a bay window and soft chairs, the way the community has healed around honest burial rites. I think about how a man who never raised his voice, who kept his warnings in a leather notebook and his legal armor in a trust, managed to outsmart a schemer by writing a few sentences and consulting a decent attorney. The moral I carry now, etched into every contract I sign and every hand I shake, is that true integrity isn’t loud. It’s a box in a desk, a warning scrawled in the margins, a lock picked in the dark by a woman who was underestimated, a signature that means exactly what it says.
